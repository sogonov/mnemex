# The Essays or Counsels, Civil and Moral — Francis Bacon (1625)

> Fifty-eight compact, aphoristic essays that dispense worldly counsel on how a prudent person navigates ambition, study, human nature, and public life.

## Author's thesis
Bacon offers not a systematic ethics but tested, worldly counsel — how to act well amid the frictions of court, business, and self. Knowledge and character are instrumental: studies "serve for delight, for ornament, and for ability," but they are perfected by experience, not by rules alone. Human nature is stubborn and calculating — self-love, ambition, and appetite drive men — and the wise person masters these tendencies through custom and habit rather than by force or preaching. The essays prize prudence, moderation, and observation over passion and idealism, treating conduct as a craft to be honed.

## Key concepts introduced or developed
- [[Practical-Wisdom]] — worldly prudence learned by observation, "above" the books that teach it
- [[Studies]] — reading, conference, and writing as complementary formers of the mind
- [[Ambition]] — a useful but dangerous humor that must be kept "progressive and not retrograde"
- [[Human-Nature]] — self-interested and habit-bound; "seldom extinguished," only subdued by custom
- [[Custom-and-Habit]] — the true governor of nature, superior to force or doctrine

## Extracted claims
- Studies serve for delight, ornament, and ability; but "crafty men contemn studies, simple men admire them, and wise men use them," for their proper use is a wisdom won by observation, above the books themselves. ^[raw/books/bacon-essays/book.md:4309-4322]
- One should read "not to contradict and confute; nor to believe and take for granted... but to weigh and consider"; some books are to be tasted, others swallowed, and a few chewed and digested. ^[raw/books/bacon-essays/book.md:4322-4328]
- Reading makes a full man, conference a ready man, and writing an exact man — so a man who writes little needs great memory, and one who reads little needs cunning to seem to know what he does not. ^[raw/books/bacon-essays/book.md:4331-4335]
- Ambition is like choler, a humor that makes men active if it has its way but "malign and venomous" if checked; princes should keep ambitious men progressive and not retrograde, or not use such natures at all. ^[raw/books/bacon-essays/book.md:3287-3301]
- Nature is often hidden, sometimes overcome, but seldom extinguished: force makes it more violent in return and doctrine only tempers it, but "custom only doth alter and subdue nature." ^[raw/books/bacon-essays/book.md:3406-3415]
- Revenge is "a kind of wild justice" that puts the law out of office; in taking revenge a man is but even with his enemy, while in passing it over he is superior, "for it is a prince's part to pardon." ^[raw/books/bacon-essays/book.md:417-422]

## Contrasted with
- [[Meditations-Marcus-Aurelius]] — Bacon's outward-facing worldly prudence and management of ambition against Marcus's inward Stoic renunciation of ambition and reputation.
- [[Walden-Thoreau]] — Bacon studies men to succeed within court and business; Thoreau withdraws from that social machinery as corrupting.
- [[Essays-Emerson]] — Bacon prizes custom, observation, and conformity to worldly counsel; Emerson exalts self-reliant nonconformity against inherited custom.

## See also
- [[Essays-Montaigne]] — the other founding essayist, sharing the aphoristic study of human folly but more introspective and skeptical than counseling.
- [[The-Prince-Machiavelli]] — a fellow manual of worldly, sometimes cold prudence for the ambitious operating in public life.
