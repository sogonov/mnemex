# The Prince — Niccolò Machiavelli (1532)

> A ruthless, realist manual on acquiring and holding political power: what a
> ruler must actually do, as opposed to what moralists say he should do.

## Bibliographic

- **Author(s):** Niccolò Machiavelli
- **Year:** written 1513, published 1532
- **Source file:** [[../../raw/books/machiavelli-the-prince/book.md]]

## Author's thesis

A prince who wishes to keep power must learn "how not to be good," and use that
knowledge according to necessity. Effective rule is judged by results — security
and the state — not by conventional virtue. Fortune governs much, but a bold
prince can master half of it.

## Key concepts introduced or developed

- [[Fear-vs-Love]] — the safer basis of a ruler's authority
- [[Fortune]] — chance in political affairs, and how far it can be mastered
- [[Virtue]] — treated instrumentally: appear virtuous, be virtuous only when useful

## Extracted claims

- It is much safer to be feared than loved, when one of the two must be dispensed with, because men are ungrateful and fickle. ^[raw/books/machiavelli-the-prince/book.md:2442-2443] → relevant to [[Fear-vs-Love]]
- Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but she still leaves us to direct the other half. ^[raw/books/machiavelli-the-prince/book.md:3453-3454] → relevant to [[Fortune]]
- Cruelty is "properly used" when applied at one blow, necessary to one's security, and not persisted in afterwards; "badly used" cruelties multiply with time. ^[raw/books/machiavelli-the-prince/book.md:1521-1525] → relevant to [[Virtue]]
- Mercenaries and auxiliaries are useless and dangerous; a state resting on such arms stands neither firm nor safe. ^[raw/books/machiavelli-the-prince/book.md:1860-1860] → relevant to [[Virtue]]
- A prince need not possess all the good qualities, but it is very necessary to appear to have them. ^[raw/books/machiavelli-the-prince/book.md:2576-2578] → relevant to [[Virtue]]
