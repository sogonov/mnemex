# The Autobiography of Benjamin Franklin (1791)

> Franklin's life story, famous for its practical program of self-improvement:
> a systematic, measured method for building character and good habits.

## Bibliographic

- **Author(s):** Benjamin Franklin
- **Year:** written 1771–1790, published 1791
- **Source file:** [[../../raw/books/franklin-autobiography/book.md]]

## Author's thesis

Character can be engineered. Franklin sets out a bold project of "moral
perfection," reduces it to thirteen concrete virtues, and works them like a
discipline — one at a time, tracked daily, ordered by a fixed daily schedule.

## Key concepts introduced or developed

- [[Thirteen-Virtues]] — the tracked habit system
- [[Virtue]] — pursued deliberately as an end and a practice
- [[Order]] — a scheme of employment for the hours of the day

## Extracted claims

- To pursue moral perfection Franklin made a little book with a page for each of the thirteen virtues, ruled into seven day-columns crossed by thirteen red lines to mark daily faults. ^[raw/books/franklin-autobiography/book.md:3589-3592] → relevant to [[Thirteen-Virtues]]
- He gave a week's strict attention to each virtue in turn, guarding one at a time and letting the others take their ordinary chance. ^[raw/books/franklin-autobiography/book.md:3647-3649] → relevant to [[Thirteen-Virtues]]
- The precept of Order required that every part of his business have its allotted time; one page held a scheme of employment for the twenty-four hours of a natural day. ^[raw/books/franklin-autobiography/book.md:3713-3716] → relevant to [[Order]]
- After a friend told him he was thought proud and overbearing, Franklin added Humility to his list, resolving to imitate Jesus and Socrates. ^[raw/books/franklin-autobiography/book.md:3905-3910] → relevant to [[Virtue]]
