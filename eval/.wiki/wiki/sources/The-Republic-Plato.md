# The Republic — Plato (c. 380 BC, trans. Benjamin Jowett)

> A dialogue that seeks to define justice by building an ideal city in speech, then reading the same threefold order back into the individual soul.

## Author's thesis
Plato (through Socrates) argues that justice is not convention, self-interest, or the advantage of the stronger, but a structural harmony: it is each part doing its own proper work and not meddling with the others. He constructs an ideal state of three classes — producers, guardian-warriors, and ruling guardians — and claims the same tripartite structure exists in the soul as reason, spirit, and appetite. A person or city is just when reason rules, spirit supports it, and appetite obeys. Because only those who know the Form of the Good can rule well, Plato concludes that cities will never escape their evils until philosophers become kings. The allegory of the cave dramatizes the philosopher's ascent from shadows to the light of true knowledge and his duty to return and govern.

## Key concepts introduced or developed
- [[Justice]] — each element doing its own work and not being a busybody.
- [[Tripartite-Soul]] — reason, spirit (passion), and appetite as three principles of the soul.
- [[Philosopher-King]] — political power and philosophy must meet in one person.
- [[Ideal-State]] — a city of three classes mirroring the ordered soul.
- [[Allegory-of-the-Cave]] — the ascent from illusion to knowledge of the Good.
- [[Form-of-the-Good]] — the highest object of knowledge that qualifies one to rule.

## Extracted claims
- Justice is the quality of every member of the city — man, woman, artisan, ruler, subject — each doing his own work and not being a busybody; this power to do one's own work is what competes with and completes wisdom, temperance, and courage. ^[raw/books/plato-republic/book.md:14466-14479]
- The individual soul has the same three principles found in the State, so the person can be described in the same terms as the city. ^[raw/books/plato-republic/book.md:14575-14583]
- Temperance and justice consist in a friendly harmony where the one ruling principle of reason and the two subject principles of spirit and desire all agree that reason ought to rule and do not rebel. ^[raw/books/plato-republic/book.md:15013-15024]
- Until philosophers are kings, or kings and princes acquire the spirit and power of philosophy so that political greatness and wisdom meet in one, cities will never rest from their evils. ^[raw/books/plato-republic/book.md:16740-16750]
- Human beings are like prisoners chained in an underground den who can see only the shadows cast on the wall by a fire behind them, mistaking these shadows for reality. ^[raw/books/plato-republic/book.md:18773-18795]

## Contrasted with
- [[The-Prince-Machiavelli]] — Machiavelli detaches statecraft from the philosopher's pursuit of the Good, holding a ruler must learn how not to be good, where Plato roots legitimate rule in knowledge of justice and the Good.
- [[Leviathan-Hobbes]] — Hobbes grounds the state in a covenant among self-interested equals seeking security, not in a natural hierarchy of souls governed by philosopher-kings.
- [[On-Liberty-Mill]] — Mill defends the individual's liberty to choose his own life against social prescription, whereas Plato subordinates the individual to the ordered, class-assigned harmony of the state.
