# Essays: Second Series — Ralph Waldo Emerson (1844)

> Emerson argues that all authority — of society, the state, and taste — is legitimate only insofar as it is an expression of the character of self-reliant individuals, and that nature is the medium in which that individual soul recovers itself.

## Author's thesis
For Emerson, the individual soul is the primary unit of value and the true source of all institutions: law, custom, and the State are "but a shabby imitation" of the private character they exist to educe. Conformity is a moral failing — the "nonconformist" who refuses conventional opinion is the one who actually helps the world, because he re-illuminates "the untried and unknown." Self-reliance, sanely proportioned, is what commands respect even from fashion, since strong will and character "make their own forms every moment." Nature functions not as scenery but as a sacrament that draws the person out of "village politics and personalities" back into solitude and the soul. The end of all cultivation is the appearance of the wise, self-governing individual, at whose maturity the coercive State becomes unnecessary.

## Key concepts introduced or developed
- [[Self-Reliance]] — trusting one's own sense of propriety over any external deference.
- [[Nonconformity]] — refusal of conventional opinion as a productive social force.
- [[Character]] — the "centrality" and self-moved substance from which a person's forms flow.
- [[Nature-as-Sacrament]] — the natural world as the medium that restores the individual to solitude and the soul.
- [[Minimal-Government]] — the claim that the wise man makes the State unnecessary.

## Extracted claims
- The nonconformist who refuses conventional opinions and practices "remains a goad and remembrancer," helping society precisely by being a problem it can neither ignore nor absorb. ^[raw/books/emerson-essays/book.md:1858-1875]
- Manners have no fixed ritual: "the laws of behavior yield to the energy of the individual," and good sense and character "make their own forms every moment," so that strong will is always in fashion. ^[raw/books/emerson-essays/book.md:2430-2439]
- We so love self-reliance that we forgive a man many sins if he shows complete self-content asking "no leave to be"; but any deference to an eminent person "forfeits all privilege of nobility." ^[raw/books/emerson-essays/book.md:2443-2447]
- Nature calls us out of "village politics and personalities" into "a delicate realm of sunset and moonlight" — the natural object restores solitude and the soul rather than serving mere utility. ^[raw/books/emerson-essays/book.md:3116-3123]
- The State must follow, not lead, the character of the citizen; therefore "the less government we have the better," since the antidote to formal government is the growth of the individual, and "the appearance of character makes the State unnecessary." ^[raw/books/emerson-essays/book.md:3629-3632]
- "The wise man is the State" — needing no army, statute-book, or church, because private character supersedes the institutions that are only its "shabby imitation." ^[raw/books/emerson-essays/book.md:3922-3932]

## Contrasted with
- [[Leviathan-Hobbes]] — Hobbes grounds order in an all-powerful sovereign because men left to themselves fall into war; Emerson holds "the less government we have the better" and the wise man renders the State unnecessary.
- [[The-Republic-Plato]] — Plato engineers justice through a hierarchical ideal state ruled by philosopher-kings; Emerson makes the State expire at the appearance of the self-governing individual.
- [[The-Prince-Machiavelli]] — Machiavelli's virtù is the manipulation of institutions and appearances for power; Emerson locates all real power in private character that "asks no leave to be."
