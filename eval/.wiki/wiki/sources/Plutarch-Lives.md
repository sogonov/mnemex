# Plutarch: Lives of the Noble Grecians and Romans — Plutarch (c. 100 CE)

> Paired biographies of Greek and Roman statesmen and generals, written to read character through conduct and to make virtue contagious by example.

## Author's thesis
Plutarch insists he is writing *lives*, not *histories*: his aim is not to chronicle every campaign but to expose the moral character of great men, because a passing jest or private act often reveals the soul more sharply than the bloodiest battle. He pairs a Greek and a Roman (Alexander with Caesar, Pericles with Fabius) and compares them, treating each life as a specimen of virtue or vice under the pressure of power and fortune. The purpose is frankly ethical: virtue, merely by being narrated, kindles in the reader an impulse to imitate it, so biography becomes a "looking-glass" in which one adjusts one's own life. Character, for Plutarch, is the stable thing — it holds constant while fortune keeps shifting — and it is the true measure of a leader.

## Key concepts introduced or developed
- [[Character-as-Destiny]] — the enduring temper of the soul, not accidents of fortune, defines a man.
- [[Exemplary-Biography]] — lives written as moral mirrors to provoke imitation of virtue.
- [[Parallel-Lives]] — pairing a Greek and Roman figure to compare character across cultures.
- [[Virtue-and-Vice-in-Leadership]] — power as the test that discloses a statesman's real nature.
- [[Self-Command]] — mastery over appetite, wealth, and ambition as the root of fitness to rule.

## Extracted claims
- Plutarch declares his design is "not to write histories, but lives," because the greatest exploits do not always reveal virtue or vice, whereas a small thing — an expression or a jest — often shows character better than the most famous sieges or bloodiest battles. ^[raw/books/plutarch-lives/book.md:41648-41661]
- Like a portrait-painter who dwells on the face where character shows, Plutarch attends to "the marks and indications of the souls of men" and leaves great battles to be treated by others. ^[raw/books/plutarch-lives/book.md:41655-41661]
- Virtue, by the bare statement of its actions, so affects the mind as to create both admiration and a desire to imitate; "moral good is a practical stimulus," inspiring an impulse to practice the moment it is seen. ^[raw/books/plutarch-lives/book.md:9177-9185]
- Plutarch writes biography as a mirror for his own conduct: the virtues of great men serve "as a sort of looking-glass, in which I may see how to adjust and adorn my own life," habituating the memory to retain images of the worthiest characters. ^[raw/books/plutarch-lives/book.md:14720-14744]
- The temper and bent of the soul remains constant while fortune continually varies — enmity, skill, and disposition do not impair with age as bodily strength does. ^[raw/books/plutarch-lives/book.md:23437-23441]
- Lysander himself endured poverty well and kept not a drachma, yet he flooded Sparta with gold and the love of it — a leader's private virtue does not guarantee the health of the state he shapes. ^[raw/books/plutarch-lives/book.md:26634-26639]

## Contrasted with
- [[The-Prince-Machiavelli]] — Machiavelli judges a ruler by effective results and lets virtù bend to fortuna and even embrace deception, where Plutarch makes settled moral character the true measure of the statesman.
- [[On-War-Clausewitz]] — Clausewitz treats war impersonally as friction and the continuation of policy, whereas Plutarch locates the decisive variable in the individual general's character.
