# On Liberty — John Stuart Mill (1859)

> A defense of the individual's sovereignty over their own mind and body, and of free discussion, against the coercive weight of law and majority opinion.

## Author's thesis
Mill argues that the only legitimate ground for society to coerce an individual is to prevent harm to others; a person's own good is never a sufficient warrant. He warns that democracy brings a new danger — the "tyranny of the majority" — where prevailing opinion enslaves the soul more deeply than any magistrate. Free discussion is indispensable because silencing any opinion robs humanity: if the view is true we lose truth, if false we lose the vivid apprehension of truth won by its collision with error. Even a true belief, held without being challenged, decays into "dead dogma." Individuality and "experiments of living" are for Mill not merely tolerable but the chief ingredient of individual and social progress.

## Key concepts introduced or developed
- [[Harm-Principle]] — coercion is justified only to prevent harm to others.
- [[Tyranny-of-the-Majority]] — social opinion can oppress more subtly than the state.
- [[Freedom-of-Speech]] — silencing any opinion robs the whole human race.
- [[Individuality]] — free self-development as the engine of progress.
- [[Self-Sovereignty]] — over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign.

## Extracted claims
- The sole end for which mankind may interfere with the liberty of any of their number is self-protection; power may be rightfully exercised over a person against their will only to prevent harm to others. ^[raw/books/mill-on-liberty/book.md:812-819]
- A person's own good, physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant for compulsion; over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign. ^[raw/books/mill-on-liberty/book.md:816-829]
- Society itself can be the tyrant: the tyranny of prevailing opinion penetrates deeper into life than political oppression, "enslaving the soul itself," so protection is needed against social as well as legal coercion. ^[raw/books/mill-on-liberty/book.md:627-645]
- The peculiar evil of silencing an opinion is that it robs the human race: if the opinion is right we lose truth for error; if wrong, we lose the clearer perception of truth produced by its collision with error. ^[raw/books/mill-on-liberty/book.md:1045-1052]
- A true opinion not fully, frequently, and fearlessly discussed is held merely as a dead dogma, not a living truth. ^[raw/books/mill-on-liberty/book.md:1671-1674]
- Individuality — different experiments of living, short of injury to others — is one of the principal ingredients of human happiness and the chief ingredient of individual and social progress. ^[raw/books/mill-on-liberty/book.md:2517-2526]

## Contrasted with
- [[Leviathan-Hobbes]] — Hobbes subordinates individual liberty to an absolute sovereign for the sake of security, where Mill fences off a sovereign private sphere the state may not touch.
- [[The-Republic-Plato]] — Plato orders society by an expert elite prescribing each person's role, against Mill's insistence that individuals judge their own good and try their own experiments of living.
- [[The-Prince-Machiavelli]] — Machiavelli measures conduct by the ruler's grip on power, whereas Mill measures the state by how little it coerces the individual.

## See also
- [[Essays-Emerson]] — Emerson's self-reliance and nonconformity echo Mill's defense of individuality against custom.
- [[Walden-Thoreau]] — Thoreau's deliberate, self-directed life and resistance to the herd complement Mill's harm principle.
- [[Wealth-of-Nations-Smith]] — Smith's case for letting self-interest operate freely parallels Mill's argument for a protected sphere of individual liberty.

## Builds on
- [[Nicomachean-Ethics-Aristotle]] — Mill's claim that flourishing requires self-cultivated character extends Aristotle's link between virtue, habituation, and eudaimonia.
