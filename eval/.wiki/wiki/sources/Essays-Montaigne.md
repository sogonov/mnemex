# Essays — Michel de Montaigne (1580)

> A skeptic turns his own mind into the laboratory: not "what is true?" but "What do I know?" — and finds humility, moderation, and the ordinary human condition to be the only honest ground.

## Author's thesis
Montaigne argues that certain knowledge is beyond us, so the honest posture is suspended judgment (Pyrrhonian doubt) crystallized in his motto "What do I know?" He treats his own mind as the one subject he can actually study, holding that "every man carries the entire form of human condition," so self-portraiture is a universal inquiry. Human presumption — the vice of taking one's opinions as the measure of possibility — is the root folly he keeps exposing. Against it he sets moderation ("Ne quid nimis," nothing in excess) and a candid acceptance of our animal, fallible nature. Wisdom, for him, is learning "how loyally to enjoy his being" within the common human model rather than escaping it.

## Key concepts introduced or developed
- [[Pyrrhonian-Skepticism]] — suspended judgment; the doubt that even doubts itself ("What do I know?").
- [[Self-Knowledge]] — the self as the primary and most reliable object of study.
- [[Moderation]] — "Ne quid nimis"; excess even in virtue and judgment is a fault.
- [[Human-Presumption]] — mistaking one's own opinion for the bounds of the possible.
- [[The-Essay]] — the trial/attempt form: thought caught in motion, "always learning and making trial."

## Extracted claims
- Skepticism cannot even be stated in ordinary affirmative language; the Pyrrhonist's whole stance collapses into a single self-cancelling question, "What do I know?", which Montaigne adopts as his emblem with a balance. ^[raw/books/montaigne-essays/book.md:24657-24671]
- To dismiss things as impossible merely because they seem improbable is "a temerarious presumption to pretend to know the utmost bounds of possibility"; the remedy is the rule "Ne quid nimis" — neither believing rashly nor being too incredulous. ^[raw/books/montaigne-essays/book.md:9164-9172]
- It is "a foolish presumption to slight and condemn all things for false that do not appear to us probable," the ordinary vice of those who fancy themselves wiser than their neighbours. ^[raw/books/montaigne-essays/book.md:9094-9098]
- Since "every man carries the entire form of human condition," an ordinary private life is as fit a subject for moral philosophy as any grand one, and Montaigne presents himself by his "universal being," faulting others for not thinking of themselves. ^[raw/books/montaigne-essays/book.md:36830-36837]
- Wisdom is knowing "how loyally to enjoy his being": however elevated our station, "seated upon the most elevated throne in the world, we are but seated upon our breech," so the fairest lives accommodate the common human model "without miracle, without extravagance." ^[raw/books/montaigne-essays/book.md:50851-50860]

## Contrasted with
- [[The-Prince-Machiavelli]] — Machiavelli prescribes power with confident certainty about how men act; Montaigne distrusts exactly that certainty and suspends judgment.
- [[Essays-Bacon]] — Bacon trusts a method that advances human knowledge; Montaigne doubts reason's reach and mocks presumption to know.
- [[Meditations-Marcus-Aurelius]] — the Stoic rests on reason's firm doctrine and rational order; Montaigne's Pyrrhonism holds even reason itself in doubt.
