# The Nicomachean Ethics — Aristotle (c. 350 BCE)

> Ethics is the study of the human good, and the human good turns out to be a life of activity in accordance with virtue — virtue itself being a settled disposition, formed by habit, that hits the mean between excess and defect.

## Author's thesis
Every action and pursuit aims at some good, and there is one chief good — happiness (*eudaimonia*) — that is sought for its own sake and never as a means to anything else. This happiness is not a feeling or a possession but an activity: the exercise of the soul's distinctively human function (reason) in accordance with excellence, sustained over a complete life. Moral virtue is not innate; nature gives us only the capacity, and we are perfected in virtue through habituation — by repeatedly performing just, brave, and temperate acts. Each virtue is a mean state relative to ourselves, lying between an excess and a defect, and determined by reason as the person of practical wisdom would determine it. Because hitting the mean at the right time, toward the right person, in the right way is difficult, goodness is rare and praiseworthy.

## Key concepts introduced or developed
- [[Eudaimonia]] — happiness as the final, self-sufficient chief good of human life.
- [[Golden-Mean]] — virtue as a mean between excess and defect, relative to the individual.
- [[Habituation]] — moral character formed by the repeated doing of virtuous acts.
- [[Function-Argument]] — the human good is the soul's activity in accordance with reason and excellence.
- [[Practical-Wisdom]] — the reasoned capacity that determines where the mean lies in each case.

## Extracted claims
- The chief good is that at which all things aim; a common and not bad description of the Chief Good is "that which all things aim at." ^[raw/books/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics/book.md:871-872]
- The good (happiness) is something final and self-sufficient — we choose it always for its own sake and never for the sake of something else, whereas honour, pleasure and the rest we choose partly with a view to happiness. ^[raw/books/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics/book.md:1200-1218]
- The Good of Man is a working of the soul in the way of excellence, sustained over a complete life — for one swallow or one fine day does not make a spring, nor does one day make a man happy. ^[raw/books/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics/book.md:1280-1286]
- The moral virtues come to be in us neither by nature nor against nature: nature gives the capacity for receiving them, and they are perfected through custom — by doing just actions we become just, by doing brave actions brave. ^[raw/books/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics/book.md:1778-1794]
- Virtue is a state apt to exercise deliberate choice, lying in the mean relative to ourselves, determined by reason as the man of practical wisdom would determine it — a middle state between two faults, one of excess and one of defect. ^[raw/books/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics/book.md:2124-2128]
- It is hard to be good: anyone can be angry or give money, but to do so toward the right person, in due proportion, at the right time, with a right object, and in the right manner is rare, and for this cause goodness is praiseworthy and noble. ^[raw/books/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics/book.md:2365-2372]

## Contrasted with
- [[Meditations-Marcus-Aurelius]] — the Stoics make virtue the *sole* good and counsel extirpation of the passions, where Aristotle moderates the passions to a mean and holds that external goods are also needed for full happiness.
- [[The-Prince-Machiavelli]] — Machiavelli's *virtù* is effectiveness in gaining and holding power, detached from moral goodness, against Aristotle's virtue as a good state of character chosen for its own sake.
- [[On-Liberty-Mill]] — Mill grounds the good in pleasure/utility and prizes individuality against social molding, where Aristotle grounds it in rational activity and relies on law and habituation to form character.
