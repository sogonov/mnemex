# An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations — Adam Smith (1776)

> A nation grows rich not by hoarding gold but by the productivity of its labour, which the division of labour, free exchange, and self-interest coordinate into unintended public benefit.

## Author's thesis
Smith argues that the real wealth of a nation is the annual produce of its labour, and that the greatest source of productivity is the division of labour, which multiplies output by specializing tasks. This division arises not from any deliberate design but from a natural human propensity to truck, barter, and exchange, and it is limited only by the extent of the market. Individuals pursuing their own gain, without intending public benefit, are led "as if by an invisible hand" to advance the good of society more effectively than those who consciously try to. From this Smith builds a case against the mercantile system of restraints and for a natural liberty of commerce in which markets, not statesmen, allocate capital to its most productive uses.

## Key concepts introduced or developed
- [[Division of labour]] — specialization of tasks that multiplies productive output.
- [[The invisible hand]] — private self-interest producing unintended public benefit.
- [[Self-interest / self-love]] — exchange rests on others' regard for their own advantage, not benevolence.
- [[Propensity to truck, barter, and exchange]] — the human disposition that gives rise to specialization.
- [[Extent of the market]] — the limit that market size places on how far labour can be divided.

## Extracted claims
- The greatest improvements in the productive powers of labour, and most of the skill and judgment applied to it, are the effects of the division of labour. ^[raw/books/smith-wealth-of-nations/book.md:225-228]
- In a pin factory, ten workers dividing the task into about eighteen operations make upward of forty-eight thousand pins a day, whereas working alone each could scarcely make twenty or even one. ^[raw/books/smith-wealth-of-nations/book.md:250-267]
- We obtain our dinner not from the benevolence of the butcher, brewer, or baker, but from their regard to their own interest; we address ourselves to their self-love, never to their humanity. ^[raw/books/smith-wealth-of-nations/book.md:575-578]
- Pursuing only his own gain, the individual is led by an invisible hand to promote an end that was no part of his intention, frequently advancing society's interest more effectively than if he had intended to. ^[raw/books/smith-wealth-of-nations/book.md:15919-15927]
- Because exchange gives occasion to the division of labour, that division is always limited by the extent of the market. ^[raw/books/smith-wealth-of-nations/book.md:668-671]

## Contrasted with
- [[The-Republic-Plato]] — Plato entrusts allocation and the good life to philosopher-kings governing from above, whereas Smith decentralizes it to self-interested individuals coordinated by the market.
- [[Leviathan-Hobbes]] — Hobbes makes an all-powerful sovereign the precondition of peace and order; Smith shows economic order emerging spontaneously without central direction.
- [[Walden-Thoreau]] — Thoreau exalts self-sufficient simple living and disdains commerce and the specialization Smith praises as the engine of wealth.
